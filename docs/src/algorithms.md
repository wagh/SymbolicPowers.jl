# Algorithms and guarantees

## Selection and hypotheses

| Method | Required hypotheses | Computation |
|:--|:--|:--|
| `:auto` | Polynomial ideal over a supported field | Selects monomial, dimension-one saturation, unmixed hull, or primary decomposition |
| `:primary` | General supported polynomial ideal | Decompose the ordinary power and retain components selected by localization |
| `:monomial` | Monomial ideal | Intersect powers of contractions to the relevant coordinate primes |
| `:saturation` | Homogeneous for positive integer weights; quotient dimension one | Saturate the ordinary power at the irrelevant ideal, subject to embedded-prime convention; for rational points, intersect powers of the point ideals |
| `:unmixed` | Every associated prime has the same height | Equidimensional hull of the ordinary power |
| `:complete_intersection` | Every primary component is certified generated by codimension-many elements; all at the same height | Intersection of component powers |
| `:join` | Radical ideal; coefficient field characteristic zero or finite | Eliminate in a doubled variable ring |
| `:frobenius` | Prime ideal; positive characteristic | A Frobenius power followed by one ideal quotient |

With `method = :auto` the first applicable of `:monomial`, `:saturation`,
`:unmixed` and `:primary` is used (only `:monomial`, `:saturation` and
`:primary` for the minimal-prime convention). Homogeneity is decided for the ideal, not for the
given generators: if these are not homogeneous, a reduced Gröbner basis is
inspected, which for a homogeneous ideal consists of homogeneous polynomials
for every monomial ordering.

`:complete_intersection` is a sufficient-hypothesis shortcut. The ungraded
`small_generating_set` heuristic may fail to certify a complete intersection;
such a rejection does not prove that it is not a complete intersection.
An explicit unsupported method raises an error rather than silently returning
another method's result. Zeroth powers and zero/unit ideals have their
mathematically fixed values before method-specific checks.

## Why the primary method is correct

If ``I^n=\bigcap_j Q_j`` and ``\sqrt{Q_j}=P_j``, localization at a prime
``M`` kills precisely the components with ``P_j\not\subseteq M``. For a
surviving primary component, contraction after localization returns ``Q_j``:
if ``sf\in Q_j`` and ``s\notin M``, then ``s\notin P_j`` and primaryness
implies ``f\in Q_j``. Consequently

```math
I^nR_M\cap R=\bigcap_{P_j\subseteq M}Q_j.
```

The final intersection therefore retains a component if its prime is contained
in **at least one** selected prime. For the default definition it suffices to
select inclusion-maximal associated primes of ``I``; the others give redundant
contractions. For the alternative definition select the minimal primes.
Testing equality of primes, instead of containment, would be incorrect in the
presence of embedded associated primes.

### Number-field decompositions

OSCAR documents that primary decompositions over number fields can contain
redundant components. A radical of a redundant component need not be an
associated prime. Merely removing duplicate radicals does not fix this.

For number-field coefficients the package first groups equal radicals (the
intersection of ``P``-primary ideals is again ``P``-primary), discards unit
components, and removes a component ``Q_i`` whenever the intersection of the
other components is contained in ``Q_i``. Each removal preserves the ideal.
The resulting decomposition is irredundant with distinct radicals, so those
radicals are exactly the associated primes. The normalized decomposition is
cached separately; OSCAR's own cached data are left intact. Over other fields
the package uses OSCAR's minimal primary decomposition directly.

Normalization is only needed where associated primes are read off: the
defining primes of ``I``, big heights, heights of associated primes, the
complete-intersection test and `is_symbolic_equal_ordinary`. The selection of
components of ``I^n`` in the primary method is valid for **every** primary
decomposition ``I^n=\bigcap_j Q_j``, redundant or not: localization commutes
with finite intersections, so ``I^nR_M\cap R=\bigcap_{P_j\subseteq M}Q_j``
by the argument above. The decomposition of ``I^n``, which is usually much
larger than that of ``I``, is therefore used as OSCAR returns it.

For example, the triangle ideal ``I=(xy,xz,yz)`` is already contained in
``(x,y,z)^2``. Appending that primary ideal to a decomposition of ``I`` is a
valid redundant decomposition, but its radical ``(x,y,z)`` is not associated
to ``R/I``. Treating it as associated would incorrectly give
``\operatorname{bight}(I)=3`` and ``I^{(2)}=I^2`` in the primary method.

## Monomial method

The contraction ``IR_P\cap R`` for a coordinate associated prime is obtained
by inverting variables outside ``P``. It is generated by monomials supported
inside ``P``. Its ordinary powers are already contracted from that localization.
Thus powers can be taken after contracting, and the relevant contractions can
then be intersected. Squarefree monomial ideals have coordinate prime primary
components, giving the familiar intersection ``\bigcap_P P^n``.

Minimal primes and localized components are independent of ``n``. The package
keeps them on the input ideal object for reuse across exponents. Localized
components for the two symbolic conventions are cached separately.

## Dimension-one saturation and unmixed ideals

With positive weights and quotient dimension one, the only possible embedded
homogeneous prime is the irrelevant maximal ideal ``\mathfrak m``. If it is
already associated to ``I``, the default symbolic power is ``I^n``: every
associated prime of its powers lies inside ``\mathfrak m``. Otherwise
saturation removes precisely the unwanted ``\mathfrak m`` component. The
minimal-prime convention always removes that embedded component. Both
conventions are therefore available, and `:auto` uses this method for both.
The test whether ``\mathfrak m`` is associated, i.e. whether
``I:\mathfrak m^\infty=I``, is cached on the ideal, together with the
saturation itself.

If the given generators show that ``I`` is a complete intersection (there
are at most ``\dim R-1`` of them), they form a regular sequence in
``R_{\mathfrak m}``. Then every ``I^k/I^{k+1}`` is free over the
Cohen–Macaulay ring ``R_{\mathfrak m}/I``, so ``R_{\mathfrak m}/I^n`` has
positive depth, ``\mathfrak m`` is not associated to ``I^n``, and
``I^{(n)}=I^n`` for both conventions. Collinear points and grids are
examples. A minimal generating set is not computed: it costs more than the
saturation of a complete intersection power, which is already saturated.

### Rational points

Every minimal prime ``P_1,\dots,P_k`` of such an ideal has dimension one. If
each ``P_i`` is generated by linear forms (the ideal of a ``K``-rational
point, also in weighted projective space when the forms involve variables of
equal weight), then after a linear change of coordinates
``P_i=(x_1,\dots,x_h)``. Its powers are monomial ideals with the single
associated prime ``P_i``, so ``P_i^k`` is ``P_i``-primary over every field.
Suppose moreover that ``I:\mathfrak m^\infty=\bigcap_i P_i^{k_i}`` (a scheme of
fat rational points; ``k_i=1`` for radical ideals). The ``P_i`` are pairwise
incomparable, so localizing at ``P_i`` discards the other components,
``I^nR_{P_i}=P_i^{nk_i}R_{P_i}``, and for the minimal-prime convention, or when
``\mathfrak m`` is not associated,

```math
I^{(n)}=\bigcap_i \left(I^nR_{P_i}\cap R\right)=\bigcap_i P_i^{nk_i}.
```

This intersection is computed two ideals at a time; a single `intersect` call
on all powers, or a balanced tree of intersections, is much slower. On the
benchmark configurations it is 5 to 50 times faster than saturating ``I^n``:
for 8 points in ``\mathbb P^2`` and ``n=6`` it takes about 0.7 s instead of
30 s, for 10 points and ``n=5`` 0.9 s instead of 29 s, and for 7 points in
``\mathbb P^3`` and ``n=4`` 0.4 s instead of 14 s.

The candidate primes are found without a primary decomposition, by
projections. For variables ``x_i,x_j`` of equal weight let ``f`` be the gcd of
the generators of ``I\cap K[x_i,x_j]``. If all minimal primes are generated by
linear forms, ``f`` is a product of linear forms (its radical is the product of
the linear forms ``P\cap K[x_i,x_j]``), so if ``f`` does not split over ``K``
the method is not applicable. Otherwise the points over a root ``(a:1)`` form
the ideal ``(I+(x_i-ax_j)):x_j^\infty``, those over ``(1:0)`` the ideal
``(I+(x_j)):x_i^\infty``, and those in the centre ``x_i=x_j=0`` the ideal
``(I+(x_i,x_j)):\mathfrak m^\infty``. Each of these that is not already the
ideal of one rational point is split with the next pair of variables; this
also separates points with the same image, and repeated hyperplane sections
reduce fat points to their supports: with all weights equal, every pair of
variables is used, and the linear forms added along the way span the linear
forms vanishing at the point. Only roots of univariate polynomials are
needed. For 6 to 10 points this takes 1 to 5 ms, against 30 to 160 ms for
`minimal_primes`. With other weights the projections give no answer for
points that are not cut out by linear forms (and when no two variables have
the same weight); then the saturation is used.

The shortcut is used over ``\mathbb Q`` and finite fields. Over number fields
it would pay off for larger examples (8 points over ``\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)``,
``n=3``: 1.4 s instead of 8 s), but the first call of `roots` (or `factor`)
over a number field in a session compiles for about a minute, which is longer
than the saturations it replaces in typical use, so the saturation is used.

Nothing found in this way is trusted. Every candidate ``P`` is checked to be
generated by ``\dim R-1`` linear forms and to contain ``I``, so it is a minimal
prime. The exponent ``k_P`` is the largest ``k`` with
``J=I:\mathfrak m^\infty\subseteq P^k``, and the inclusion
``\bigcap_P P^{k_P}\subseteq J`` is checked (first with all ``k_P=1``). The
reverse inclusion holds by construction, and no minimal prime ``Q`` can be
missing: ``J\subseteq Q`` would give ``P^{k_P}\subseteq Q`` for some ``P``, so
``P=Q``. If a check fails, the saturation is used. The data are cached on the
ideal, as is the test whether ``\mathfrak m`` is associated.

The shortcut is not used for other minimal primes. Powers of a non-complete
intersection prime (for example a Galois orbit of three non-collinear points)
need not be primary. For complete intersection primes ``P^n`` is
``P``-primary, but the primes need a decomposition, and the intersection is
not reliably faster: for three Galois orbits and a rational point it took
2.4 s instead of 10.7 s (``n=4``), but for the Fermat configuration, whose
sparse ideal ``I^n`` Singular saturates quickly, 0.25 s instead of 0.02 s
(``n=5``, including the decomposition). Such configurations are rejected by
the first projection in well under a millisecond on the benchmarks. Fat points whose primary components are not
powers of their primes, such as a curvilinear double point ``(x^2,y)``, also
use the saturation. These restrictions are performance choices; the
saturation is correct in all cases.

Two other ways to speed up the saturation were measured and not adopted.
Bayer–Stillman saturation, ``I^n:\mathfrak m^\infty=I^n:\ell^\infty`` for a
linear form ``\ell`` outside every minimal prime, computed from one
degree-reverse-lexicographic Gröbner basis with ``\ell`` as the last variable,
needs a coordinate change when no variable avoids all points. This happens,
for instance, whenever the coordinate points are among them. With Singular's
`std` it was 1.1 to 22 times slower than the intersection above on rational
points, and several hundred times slower than the saturation on the Fermat
quintic configuration (31 s against 0.06 s for ``n=5``).
msolve's F4 (`groebner_basis_f4`) is faster still, but over ``\mathbb Q`` it
uses a multi-modular approach without a final verification over
``\mathbb Q`` (as documented by OSCAR), so its output would not be
certified.

For an unmixed ideal of height ``h``, the primes selected by the definition
are exactly the minimal primes, all of height ``h``. The equidimensional hull
of ``I^n`` keeps precisely their primary components.

## Component complete intersections

For a primary complete intersection ``Q`` in a polynomial ring, the ordinary
powers remain primary to its radical. If all components are such complete
intersections of the same height, there are no embedded components, and
localizing at any minimal prime identifies ``I`` with the corresponding
component. Intersecting the component powers gives either symbolic convention.

## Join method and its boundary

Compute ``I(t)+J(x-t)`` in ``K[x,t]`` and eliminate ``t``. For a radical ideal
``I`` over a perfect field, the Zariski-Nagata differential characterization
gives ``I^{(n)}=I\star\mathfrak m^n``. The elimination also handles Hasse
rather than just ordinary derivatives in positive characteristic, so there is
no factorial inversion. The implemented field check accepts characteristic
zero and OSCAR's `FinField` types. It is sufficient, not an attempt to
recognize every perfect field. In particular, it does not call a potentially
unimplemented generic `is_finite` method on arbitrary positive-characteristic
fields.

For nonradical input this equality fails: ``(x^2)\star(x,y)^2=(x^3)`` whereas
``(x^2)^{(2)}=(x^4)``. `symbolic_join` is still a valid general join operation;
only its identification as a symbolic power needs radicality.

## Frobenius method

Let ``P`` be prime of height ``h``, let ``q=p^e\geq n`` and put
``a=h(q-1)-n+1``. Flatness of Frobenius in a regular polynomial ring implies
that ``P^{[q]}`` is ``P``-primary. In the regular local ring ``R_P`` the
maximal ideal is generated by a regular system of ``h`` parameters, and

```math
\mathfrak p^{[q]}:\mathfrak p^{h(q-1)-n+1}=\mathfrak p^n
\qquad(1\leq n\leq q).
```

For monomials in the parameters this follows by complementing exponents in
the box ``0\leq a_i<q``; the regular local identity follows by the associated
graded ring (or completion). Taking contractions gives
``P^{(n)}=P^{[q]}:P^a``. The zero prime and ``n=0,1`` are handled separately.
The code checks exponent arithmetic before converting to machine integers.
Primality is a necessary hypothesis.

## When is the symbolic power equal to the ordinary power?

Let ``S`` be the set of associated primes of ``I^n`` that are contained in
one of the defining primes (the maximal associated primes of ``I``, or its
minimal primes). By the description of the primary method,
``I^{(n)}=\bigcap_{P\in S}Q_P`` for a primary decomposition
``I^n=\bigcap_P Q_P``. The set ``S`` is closed under passing to smaller
associated primes, so this intersection is irredundant and its associated
primes are exactly ``S``. Hence

```math
I^{(n)}=I^n\iff S=\operatorname{Ass}(R/I^n).
```

For nonmonomial ideals, `is_symbolic_equal_ordinary` uses this prime criterion.
For monomial ideals, forming the symbolic power and testing
``I^{(n)}\subseteq I^n`` avoids decomposing ``I^n``. The reverse containment
``I^n\subseteq I^{(n)}`` holds for either convention, so this test decides
equality. For the minimal-prime convention the prime criterion says that
``I^n`` has no embedded primes.

## Containment searches

The predicate ``m\mapsto[I^{(m)}\subseteq I^n]`` is monotone, since the
symbolic powers decrease. It is false for ``m<n`` when ``I`` is nonzero and
proper (because ``I^m\subseteq I^{(m)}`` and ``I^m\not\subseteq I^n``), and
for the default convention it is true for ``m\geq hn`` with
``h=\operatorname{bight}(I)`` (Ein–Lazarsfeld–Smith, Hochster–Huneke).

`containment_problem` tests ``m=n,n+1,n+3,n+7,\ldots`` until the first
containment (or the bound ``hn``, which needs no computation), and then
bisects the last gap. The largest symbolic power computed is therefore less
than about twice the answer, and only logarithmically many containments are
tested. A plain bisection of ``[n,hn]`` would instead start with a symbolic
power of order ``hn/2``, which is expensive when the answer is close to ``n``.
Symbolic powers computed during the search are cached, and
`lower_bound_resurgence` shares this cache between the different values of
``n``.

For `in_symbolic = true` the largest ``k`` with ``I^{(n)}\subseteq I^k`` lies
in ``[\lfloor n/h\rfloor,n]`` for the default convention (because
``I^{(n)}\subseteq I^{(h\lfloor n/h\rfloor)}\subseteq I^{\lfloor n/h\rfloor}``)
and in ``[0,n]`` otherwise; it is found by bisection.

## König property and packing

For a monomial ideal with minimal generators of supports
``E_1,\ldots,E_r\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}``, the minimal primes are the
ideals ``(x_i:i\in C)`` for the minimal sets ``C`` meeting every ``E_j``.
Hence ``\operatorname{codim}(I)=\tau(E)``, the minimal size of such a set.
Nonconstant monomials form a regular sequence exactly when their supports are pairwise
disjoint, and any disjoint monomials in ``I`` are divisible by minimal
generators with disjoint supports. So ``I`` is König if and only if ``E``
contains ``\tau(E)`` pairwise disjoint sets, i.e. the matching number of the
hypergraph ``E`` equals its transversal number.

Setting the variables in ``Z`` to 0 deletes the generators meeting ``Z``;
setting the variables in ``O`` to 1 removes ``O`` from every support. Both
operations act on supports, so `is_konig`, `is_packed` and
`non_packed_substitutions` are computed entirely on the hypergraph, without
ideal arithmetic. Variables that occur in no minimal generator do not change
any minor and are not substituted.

`squarefree_in_codim` also avoids constructing an ideal power. A squarefree
monomial in ``I^c`` must be a product of ``c`` squarefree minimal generators
with pairwise disjoint supports, times another monomial. Consequently its
minimal generators are obtained by enumerating these disjoint products and
removing products whose supports contain another product's support. This last
step matters: for ``I=(xy,zw,xz,ywv)`` the disjoint product ``xz\cdot ywv``
is divisible by the other disjoint product ``xy\cdot zw``. The method applies
to nonsquarefree monomial ideals as well, because a nonsquarefree factor can
never divide a squarefree monomial.

## Backend limitations

OSCAR may invoke Singular for decomposition, membership, elimination, and
quotients. Capability and performance depend on the coefficient field and
backend version. No blanket assertion that all small prime fields fail is
made. In particular, OSCAR warns that its default GTZ decomposition may not
terminate over small finite fields; if it terminates, its result is correct.
Join and Frobenius core computations avoid primary decomposition;
their default radicality or primality checks may still invoke decomposition
routines. The direct helpers expose `check=false` for established hypotheses.

Public exponents are Julia `Int` values. Singular-backed ideal powers have
additional implementation limits (in the tested backend, a signed 32-bit
exponent); sparse polynomial powers also have backend-dependent exponent
limits. These are resource/API limits, not extensions of the mathematical
domain. The zero and unit ideals are returned directly for positive symbolic
powers, so those known answers do not invoke ideal powering.

Different methods can return different generating sets for the same ideal.
Compare results with `==`, not by comparing their generator arrays.
